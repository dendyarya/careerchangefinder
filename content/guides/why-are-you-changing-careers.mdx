If you are changing careers, you will be asked this question in almost every interview. The interviewer is not trying to catch you out. They want to know three things: that your move is thought through, that you will stay, and that your past is an asset rather than a gap.

This guide gives you a simple formula, sample answers for common switches, and the mistakes that make career changers sound unsure.

## What the interviewer really wants to know

- **Is this a considered decision?** They want to see that you researched the field, not that you are running from something.
- **Will you stay?** Hiring and training someone costs money. They want confidence you will not change your mind in six months.
- **What do you bring?** Your past work should look like a strength for this role.

A good answer addresses all three in under a minute.

## The 3-part formula

1. **Where you are coming from:** one sentence on your past work, focused on what you were good at.
2. **What drew you here:** the specific reason you chose this field, ideally something you experienced or tested.
3. **Why you fit this role:** the skills and results from your past that match the new job's main tasks.

Keep it positive and forward-looking. Talk about what you are moving toward, not what you are escaping.

## Sample answers

### Teacher to corporate trainer

> "I've spent eight years teaching high school science, and the part I loved most was designing lessons that made complicated ideas click. Over the last two years I led professional development sessions for other teachers and realized I enjoy training adults just as much. This role is focused on designing and delivering training for new employees, which uses the same skills: planning a curriculum, explaining clearly, and checking that people actually learned. I've also completed a course in instructional design to get up to speed on the corporate side."

### Retail manager to project coordinator

> "In retail management I ran schedules for 25 people, handled inventory and coordinated store remodels with contractors and head office. The remodels were my favorite part, so I looked into project coordination and earned a project management certificate this spring. This role is about keeping timelines, budgets and teams on track, which is what I've been doing for six years, now in a setting where it's the main job rather than a side task."

### Nurse to clinical educator

> "I've been an ICU nurse for nine years, and I've precepted most of the new nurses on our unit. I found that helping someone become confident is the most satisfying part of my day. I'd like to make that my full-time focus. In this role I can use my clinical knowledge and my experience training new staff to improve how people learn the work."

### Customer service to sales

> "I've worked in customer service for five years and consistently had among the highest satisfaction scores on my team. I noticed I enjoyed the calls where I could recommend an upgrade that actually solved a customer's problem. Sales lets me do that full time. I already know how to listen, handle objections and stay calm with difficult customers, which I understand are the core of this role."

## What to say if you were laid off or burned out

You do not have to share every reason. Be honest but brief, then move to the forward-looking part:

> "My role was eliminated in a reorganization last year. It gave me the push to move toward work I'd been interested in for a while, and since then I've..."

> "After ten years in that field I wanted work that used more of my strengths in [skill]. I spent the last six months testing that through [course, volunteer work, project], and it confirmed this is the right direction."

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Complaining about your old job or employer.** Even if it was bad, it makes you sound like you might complain about this one.
- **Saying it is "just something new".** It sounds like you might leave again soon.
- **Apologizing for your background.** Phrases like "I know I don't have experience, but..." undercut you before you start.
- **Talking too long.** One minute is enough. Let them ask follow-up questions.
- **Not connecting the dots.** Do not leave the interviewer to work out why your past helps. Say it.

## Find the connections in your own background

The strongest answers point to skills that both careers share. O\*NET, the U.S. Department of Labor's career database, rates 25 skills in every career, and several appear in most jobs. Judgment and decision making is rated 3 or higher out of 5 in 672 of 875 careers, coordination in 628, and time management in 648.

To find your strongest overlaps:

1. Enter your current job in the [career change tool](/career-change/tool) and open the new career from the list. The match score shows how similar the two are.
2. Compare the top skills on both career profiles.
3. Pick two shared skills and prepare one concrete result for each.

## Practice it

- Write your answer down, then cut it to about 100 words.
- Say it out loud until it sounds natural, not memorized.
- Prepare for follow-ups: "Why not stay in your field?", "What will you miss?", "How do you know you'll like this?"
- Use the same story in your [resume summary](/guides/career-change-resume) and [cover letter](/guides/career-change-cover-letter), so everything matches.

## Likely follow-up questions

Interviewers often dig a little deeper. Prepare short answers to these:

- **"Why not stay in your current field?"** Say what the new field offers that the old one could not, such as more focus on the part of the work you enjoy most.
- **"What will you miss about your old job?"** Name something genuine, then show how the new role keeps part of it.
- **"How do you know you'll like this work?"** Point to what you did to test it: a course, volunteer work, a project or conversations with people in the role.
- **"What has been hardest about the switch?"** Choose a real challenge, such as learning a new tool, and explain how you are handling it.
- **"Where do you see yourself in three years?"** Describe growth inside the new field, which shows commitment.

Keep each answer to two or three sentences. Short, confident answers show that your decision is settled.

## Common questions

### How long should my answer be?

About 45 seconds to one minute, or roughly 100 words. Long enough to cover where you came from, why this field and why you fit, short enough to leave room for questions.

### Should I mention salary as a reason?

Usually not as the main reason. If pay is part of it, frame it as long-term growth in a field you want to commit to, and lead with the work itself.

### What if I'm changing careers because of burnout?

Be brief and positive. Say you wanted work that uses different strengths, and show what you did to test the new field. You do not need to describe the burnout.

### Is it OK to say I'm still exploring?

Not in a job interview. Do your exploring before you apply, and present the switch as a decision you have made.
