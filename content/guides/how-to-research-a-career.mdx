The best time to find out that a career is not right for you is before you switch. A few hours of research and a few conversations can save you a year in the wrong job. This guide shows what to look at, where to find reliable information, and how to check that the career fits you in practice.

## The 8 things to research

For each career on your shortlist, find out:

1. **What people actually do all day.** The list of daily tasks matters more than the job title.
2. **What it pays,** at the median and at the low end where new workers start.
3. **What education and training it needs,** including licenses and certificates.
4. **How many jobs there are,** and whether the field is growing or shrinking.
5. **Where the jobs are,** since some careers are concentrated in a few states.
6. **The downsides:** stress, physical demands, schedules, travel.
7. **How close it is to your current skills,** which affects how fast you can switch.
8. **Who you would work with:** the public, patients, a small team, mostly alone.

## Where to find reliable information

### Government data

The most reliable numbers for U.S. careers come from two federal sources:

- **O\*NET**, from the U.S. Department of Labor, describes the tasks, skills, knowledge, work context and preparation for about 1,000 occupations, based on surveys of workers and experts.
- **The Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS)** publishes pay and employment for each occupation, nationally and by state, in its Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics.

Every career profile on this site combines both. For example, the profile for [registered nurses](/careers/registered-nurses) shows the daily tasks, top skills, education levels, BLS pay by state and the hard parts of the job in one page.

### People in the job

Data tells you what the job looks like on average. People tell you what it feels like. Two or three conversations with people in the career are the most valuable research you can do. See our guide to [informational interviews](/guides/informational-interviews).

### Job ads

Read 15 to 20 current job ads for the career in your area. Note the requirements that appear again and again. Those are the real entry requirements, which can differ from what any summary says.

## Step-by-step: researching a career on this site

1. **Start with your own job.** Enter it in the [career change tool](/career-change/tool) to see the 25 closest careers, with match scores, pay change and extra preparation.
2. **Open the career profile** of each option. Read the task list first. If most tasks sound unappealing, cross it off.
3. **Check the pay range.** Look at the median and the lower end. New workers usually start below the median.
4. **Check the downsides section.** Each profile lists the hard parts of the job from O\*NET work context data, such as time pressure, physical work or conflict.
5. **Check state pay** on the career's salary page if you plan to stay in one place.
6. **Compare two or three finalists side by side** using the pay, preparation and match numbers.

## How to read the key numbers

### Median pay

The median is the middle value: half of workers earn more, half less. It is more useful than the average, which very high earners pull up. The BLS also publishes the 10th and 90th percentiles, which show the range from new or low-paid workers to the top.

### Job Zone

O\*NET sorts careers into five Job Zones by how much preparation they need, from little (Zone 1) to extensive, usually a graduate degree (Zone 5). If a career is in the same zone as your current job or a lower one, you usually will not need more schooling.

### Match score

On this site, the match score (0 to 100) shows how similar two careers are in skills, knowledge, work activities and interests. Our [methodology](/methodology) explains how it is calculated. A high score means more of your experience transfers.

## Test the career before you switch

Research narrows your list. Testing decides it. Low-cost ways to test a career:

- **Shadow someone** for a day or a shift.
- **Volunteer** in a related setting, such as a hospital, school or nonprofit.
- **Take one short course** and see whether you enjoy the material.
- **Do a small project,** such as a freelance job, a sample design or a short report.
- **Ask your employer** for a task that overlaps with the new field.

After each test, write down three things you liked and three you did not. Patterns will appear quickly.

## Red flags in your research

- The tasks you would do most of the time are ones you avoid in your current job.
- Almost all job ads ask for experience you cannot get without the job itself, and there are no trainee or apprentice routes.
- The people you talk to would not choose the career again.
- The pay at the low end is below what you can live on, and the path to the median is long.

One red flag is not a dealbreaker. Two or three on the same career mean you should keep looking.

## Common questions

### What is the best website to research careers?

For U.S. careers, government sources are the most reliable: O\*NET for tasks and skills, and the BLS for pay and employment. Career Change Finder combines both and adds career change matches, so you can see how close each career is to the job you have now.

### How long should I research a career before switching?

A few weeks of focused research and two or three conversations are usually enough to narrow your list. Then test your top choice for a month or two before committing.

### What questions should I ask about a career?

Ask what a typical day looks like, what surprised people when they started, what the hardest part is, and how they got in. Our [informational interview guide](/guides/informational-interviews) has a full list.

### How do I find out if a career is in demand?

Check the number of jobs on the career profile and read current job ads in your area. Many openings and many employers hiring for the same title are good signs.
