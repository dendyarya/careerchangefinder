Job ads and career advice talk about "hard skills" and "soft skills" as if everyone agreed on what they mean. In practice, the line is blurry. But the distinction is useful for one big reason when you change careers: soft skills travel with you to almost any job, while many hard skills stay behind.

This guide explains the difference, shows how widely each kind of skill is used across U.S. careers, and explains how to present both when you switch.

## The difference in one sentence each

- **Hard skills** are specific, teachable abilities tied to tools, methods or a field: running a CNC machine, writing SQL, reading an X-ray, doing payroll.
- **Soft skills** are general ways of working with people, problems and time: explaining things clearly, making good decisions, organizing your day, persuading others.

Hard skills are often easier to test and certify. Soft skills are harder to prove but used in far more jobs.

## How O\*NET measures skills

The U.S. Department of Labor's O\*NET database does not use the words "hard" and "soft". Instead, it rates 25 cross-functional skills in every career, on a scale from 1 (not important) to 5 (extremely important), and groups them into:

- **Social skills:** coordination, instructing, negotiation, persuasion, service orientation, social perceptiveness.
- **Complex problem solving.**
- **Systems skills:** judgment and decision making, systems analysis, systems evaluation.
- **Resource management skills:** time management and managing money, materials and people.
- **Technical skills:** equipment maintenance and selection, installation, operation and control, operations analysis and monitoring, programming, quality control, repairing, technology design, troubleshooting.

Roughly, the first four groups are what most people call soft skills, and the technical group holds many of the hard ones. Field knowledge, such as medicine or law, is rated separately in O\*NET as knowledge rather than skills.

The top four are needed in more than 70% of all careers. If you are strong in them, you already meet part of the requirements of most jobs you might move into.

These skills matter a lot in the careers that use them, but each one is important in a smaller set of jobs. That makes them powerful within a field, for example troubleshooting moves well between electrician, HVAC and maintenance jobs, but less useful as a bridge into a very different field.

## What this means for a career change

1. **Lead with soft skills when the move is far.** Moving from nursing to sales or from teaching to HR, your shared skills are mostly social and thinking skills. Put them first.
2. **Lead with hard skills when the move is close.** Moving from electrician to power-line work, your technical skills carry straight over. Put them first.
3. **Close one technical gap at a time.** The [career change tool](/career-change/tool) shows the skills you would need to build for each match. Usually one or two technical skills make the difference.
4. **Prove soft skills with results.** "Strong communicator" means nothing on its own. "Trained 12 new hires, cutting their ramp-up time by a third" proves it.

## How to show hard skills

- List tools, software, equipment and methods by name.
- Add licenses and certifications with dates.
- Include a portfolio, work samples or project links where they exist.
- Match the wording in job ads, since many employers use keyword filters.

## How to show soft skills

- Use short stories in the STAR format: situation, task, action, result.
- Quantify where you can: people trained, conflicts resolved, deadlines met, customers served.
- Ask for references who can speak to how you work.
- In interviews, describe a specific decision you made and why.

## What to lead with: examples

| Career change | Lead with | Main skill to build |
|---|---|---|
| Teacher to corporate trainer | Instructing, social perceptiveness | Business knowledge, e-learning tools |
| Nurse to health informatics | Judgment, complex problem solving | Data and software skills |
| Electrician to HVAC technician | Troubleshooting, repairing | Refrigeration systems and license |
| Customer service to sales | Service orientation, persuasion | Negotiation and product knowledge |
| Accountant to financial analyst | Systems analysis, judgment | Financial modeling tools |
| Retail manager to project coordinator | Coordination, time management | Project management methods |

The pattern holds across most moves: the bigger the jump between fields, the more the soft skills carry you, and the more targeted the one or two hard skills you need to add.

## Common questions

### Which is more important, hard skills or soft skills?

It depends on the job. Hard skills often get you the interview, especially in technical fields. Soft skills such as judgment, coordination and time management are used in most careers and matter more as you move up or switch fields.

### What are examples of soft skills?

Judgment and decision making, time management, coordination with others, problem solving, reading people's reactions, helping others, teaching and persuading. O\*NET rates each of these in every U.S. career.

### Can soft skills be learned?

Yes. They improve with practice and feedback, just like technical skills. Leading a small project, training a coworker or handling customer complaints are all ways to build them.

### Which soft skills transfer to the most careers?

In O\*NET data, judgment and decision making (672 of 875 careers), time management (648), coordination (628) and complex problem solving (625) are rated at least moderately important in the most careers. See the full [transferable skills list](/guides/transferable-skills-list).
