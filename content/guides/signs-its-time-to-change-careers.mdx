Everyone has bad weeks at work. A bad week is not a reason to change careers. But when the same problems keep coming back, no matter which employer or manager you have, the issue may be the career itself rather than the job.

This guide lists the most common signs that it is time for a change, shows how to tell a bad job from the wrong career, and explains what to do once you are fairly sure.

## Bad job or wrong career?

Before you look at the signs, separate two different problems:

- **A bad job** is about one place: a difficult manager, low pay for your field, a toxic team, a long commute. Changing employers usually fixes it.
- **The wrong career** is about the work itself: the daily tasks, the skills you use, the kind of problems you solve. It follows you from employer to employer.

A simple test: imagine doing the same work at the best company in your field, with a great manager and fair pay. If that sounds good, you probably need a new job. If it still sounds draining, you may need a new career.

## 12 signs it's time to change careers

### The work itself

1. **You dread the core tasks, not just the meetings.** If the main thing your job is about, such as selling, treating patients or writing code, feels draining most days, that is a career signal.
2. **You feel nothing when you do the work well.** Success should feel at least a little rewarding. If finishing a big project feels empty, your interests may lie elsewhere.
3. **You envy people in a different field.** Pay attention to whose work you read about, ask about or watch. Envy often points to a direction.
4. **Your strongest skills are rarely used.** If you are good at teaching, analysis or building things, and your job uses none of it, you are leaving value unused.

### Your growth

5. **You stopped learning.** If the last year taught you nothing new and the next five look the same, the career may have run its course for you.
6. **The next step up does not appeal to you.** If you do not want your manager's job, or the job above that, the path ahead has no destination you care about.
7. **Your field is shrinking.** Some careers are declining because of technology or changing demand. Check the employment numbers for your career on its profile and in BLS projections.

### Your life

8. **Your health is suffering.** Constant stress, poor sleep or physical strain that does not improve with rest is a serious sign, especially in physically demanding work.
9. **The schedule no longer fits your life.** Night shifts, travel or long hours may have worked before and may not work now.
10. **The pay ceiling is too low for your goals.** If the top of your field pays less than you need, a raise will not fix it.

### Your thinking

11. **You daydream about leaving most days.** Occasional thoughts are normal. Daily planning of an exit is a strong signal.
12. **You have been "about to decide" for more than a year.** Long indecision usually means the answer is yes, but the path is unclear.

## How many signs are enough?

There is no magic number, but these rules of thumb help:

- **1 or 2 signs, mostly about the workplace:** try a new employer or a new role in your field first.
- **3 to 5 signs, including the work itself:** start exploring a change seriously.
- **6 or more signs:** you are likely in the wrong career. Plan the change now, even if it takes a year to complete.

## When it is not the right time

Changing careers is a big decision. Wait or slow down if:

- you are in a short crisis, such as a bad review or a conflict, that may pass;
- you have no savings and the new career needs unpaid training;
- you are burned out and need rest more than change; burnout can make every option look bad;
- you have not tested the new field at all.

In these cases, take small steps first: rest, save, talk to people in other careers, and take one course.

## What to do once you see the signs

### 1. Find out what you want more of

List the parts of your current job you like and the parts you avoid. Then take the free [career test](/career-test) to see which kinds of work fit your interests. The results describe the work you are drawn to, using the same Interest Profiler the U.S. Department of Labor uses.

### 2. Look at careers close to your current one

You do not have to start over. Enter your job in the [career change tool](/career-change/tool) to see the 25 careers with the most similar skills. Across all careers on this site, 88.2% of those close matches usually need no more preparation than the job you have now.

### 3. Compare pay before you decide

For most careers, a close match pays more. In our [better-paying careers study](/research/better-paying-careers), 88.5% of careers with their own BLS pay figure had a close match that pays at least 10% more at the median.

### 4. Test the new career

Volunteer, shadow someone, take one course or do a small project. A few weeks of real experience will confirm the signs or show you that the grass only looked greener.

### 5. Make a plan with a date

Set a target date and a budget. Our guide on [how long a career change takes](/guides/how-long-does-a-career-change-take) helps you build a realistic timeline.

## A 30-day check before you decide

If you are not sure whether the signs are real or just a rough patch, keep a short log for one month:

1. **Each day, rate your energy at the end of work** from 1 (drained) to 5 (energized).
2. **Write down the task that drained you most and the one you enjoyed most.**
3. **Note whether the problem was the work, the people or the conditions.**

After 30 days, look for patterns. If the same core tasks drain you week after week, the career is likely the problem. If the bad days cluster around one manager, project or schedule, a change of job or team may be enough.

The tasks you enjoyed are clues too. Search for careers built around them in the [career test](/career-test) results or the career profiles on this site.

## Common questions

### How do I know if I should change careers or just jobs?

Ask whether the problem would follow you to the best employer in your field. If yes, it is the career. If a better manager, team or pay would fix it, change jobs first.

### Is it normal to want a career change?

Yes. Americans born from 1957 to 1964 held an average of 12.9 jobs from ages 18 to 58, according to the BLS. Changing direction during a working life is common.

### What if I don't know what career I want?

Start with your interests rather than job titles. The [career test](/career-test) and the [career change quiz](/career-change/quiz) suggest careers that fit, and the career change tool shows which ones are closest to your current skills.

### Is burnout a sign I need a new career?

Not always. Burnout can come from workload or workplace problems that a new job would fix. If you still dislike the core work after rest, it may be a career issue.
