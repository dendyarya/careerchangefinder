If you are not sure which career you want, a flexible major can keep your options open. But "flexible" is often used loosely. This guide uses the official U.S. crosswalk between college programs and occupations to show which majors connect to the most careers, what that means in practice, and when a specialized major is the better choice.

## How we measured flexibility

The U.S. Department of Education and the Bureau of Labor Statistics publish a crosswalk that links each college program (a CIP code) to the occupations it prepares students for (SOC codes). We counted, for each of 1,901 majors, how many of the 875 careers with full data on this site it is linked to.

Two facts frame the results:

- The typical major is linked to **3** careers.
- About **1 in 5 majors (20.8%)** is linked to just **one** career. These are usually specialized programs, such as a specific health technology.

So a major linked to 15 or more careers is unusually broad.

### What the top majors have in common

- **Business majors** connect to management roles across every industry, from general and operations managers to sales and administrative services managers.
- **Computing majors** connect to a wide range of technology jobs: software development, systems analysis, security, databases and IT management.
- **Quantitative majors** such as mathematics, statistics and economics connect to analysis roles in science, finance, government and industry.

## A caution about the numbers

The crosswalk counts some broad links. For example, many social science majors, such as psychology, economics, political science, history and sociology, are each linked to a set of specialized management roles grouped under "managers, all other", such as compliance and regulatory affairs managers. Those links are real but usually require experience after graduation.

In practice, a liberal arts or social science major opens doors through skills, such as writing, research and analysis, more than through a direct path. That is valuable, but it often means you will need an extra step after graduation, such as an internship, a certificate or a graduate program.

## Flexible vs. specialized: which is better?

| | Flexible major | Specialized major |
|---|---|---|
| Career options | Many | Few, but clear |
| Path after graduation | Often needs an extra step | Often direct |
| Risk if you change your mind | Lower | Higher |
| Licensing | Rarely tied to one license | Often tied to a license (nursing, dental hygiene, accounting) |
| Example | Business administration | Registered nursing |

Neither is better for everyone:

- **Choose flexible** if you are unsure of your direction, want to keep options open, or plan to decide through internships.
- **Choose specialized** if you know the career you want, especially if it requires a license, such as nursing or teaching.

## How to make any major more flexible

- **Add a minor or a skills-based certificate.** A psychology major with a statistics minor connects to far more analytical roles. See [how to choose a minor](/guides/how-to-choose-a-minor).
- **Build transferable skills.** In O\*NET, judgment and decision making is rated at least moderately important in 672 of 875 careers, and time management in 648. See our [transferable skills list](/guides/transferable-skills-list).
- **Do internships in two different fields.** Real experience tells you more than any course.
- **Learn data and software tools** used across industries, such as spreadsheets and basic data analysis.

## How to check a major's career links yourself

1. Find the major in your college catalog and note its official program name.
2. Look at which careers it leads to and whether those careers need a license or graduate degree.
3. Open the profiles of those careers to compare pay, tasks and preparation.
4. Ask the department where recent graduates went to work; that is the most direct evidence.

## Common questions

### What is the most versatile college major?

By the official crosswalk, Business Administration and Management, General is linked to the most careers: 42 of the 875 with full data on this site. Computer science (25) and general computer and information sciences (23) follow.

### Is a liberal arts degree flexible?

In skills, yes; in direct career paths, less so. Liberal arts majors build writing, research and reasoning skills used in many jobs, but graduates often need an internship, certificate or graduate program to enter a specific field.

### Should I pick a broad major if I'm undecided?

It is a reasonable choice, especially in your first year. Take intro courses in a few fields, try the [college major quiz](/college-major-quiz), and narrow down by the end of your second year. See [how to choose a major](/guides/how-to-choose-a-major).

### Do specialized majors pay more?

Some do, especially in healthcare and engineering, because they lead directly to licensed or in-demand careers. But many flexible majors, such as computer science and finance, also lead to high-paying careers.
