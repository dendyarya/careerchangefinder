If you are wondering whether to change your major, you are in good company. Among students who started college in 2011–12 and declared a major, about 30% changed it at least once within three years, and about 1 in 10 changed more than once ([NCES](https://nces.ed.gov/pubs2018/2018434.pdf)). Changing is normal. What matters is changing for the right reasons and at the right time.

## How common is it, by field?

The same NCES study found big differences between fields:

| Field of the first major | Share who changed within 3 years |
|---|---|
| Mathematics | 52% |
| Natural sciences | 40% |
| Engineering | 32% |
| Computer and information sciences | 28% |
| Health care | 26% |
| Other applied fields | 22% |

Source: NCES Data Point 2018-434, students who began college in 2011–12 (associate's and bachelor's degree programs).

So if you are in math or science and having doubts, you are far from alone.

## Good reasons to change your major

- **You dislike the core subject, not just one class or professor.** If the intro and second-level courses both feel like a grind, the advanced ones rarely get better.
- **The careers it leads to do not appeal to you.** Look at the careers your major is linked to and read their profiles: daily tasks, the hard parts of the job, pay and the education most workers have. If none of them sounds like a life you want, that is a strong signal.
- **You keep struggling in the core courses** despite real effort and help, and it is affecting your grades and confidence.
- **You discovered an interest that fits you much better,** confirmed by more than one course or experience.

## Weaker reasons (pause and check first)

- **One bad semester or one hard class.** Every major has a "weed-out" course. Talk to an advisor before deciding.
- **Pressure from friends or family** in either direction.
- **A headline about a "hot" field.** Pay and demand change over time; a field you dislike is hard to finish either way.
- **Fear that your major locks you into one job.** Many careers accept graduates from many majors (see below).

## Your major may matter less than you think

Before you switch, check whether the career you want really requires a different major. In the U.S. Department of Education's crosswalk of majors to careers, many careers are linked to a wide range of majors:

| Career | Majors linked to it |
|---|---|
| [Data Scientists](/careers/data-scientists) | 35 |
| [Registered Nurses](/careers/registered-nurses) | 21 |
| [Software Developers](/careers/software-developers) | 20 |
| [Management Analysts](/careers/management-analysts) | 17 |
| [Market Research Analysts and Marketing Specialists](/careers/market-research-analysts-and-marketing-specialists) | 16 |
| [General and Operations Managers](/careers/general-and-operations-managers) | 15 |

Source: CIP 2020 to SOC 2018 crosswalk; our count of majors per career.

Sometimes adding a minor, a few electives or an internship gets you to the career you want without a full switch. Licensed careers are the exception: nursing, teaching, accounting and engineering usually require a specific program. Read more in [does your major matter](/guides/does-your-major-matter).

## What switching costs

Changing your major can cost time and money if credits do not count toward the new one. Before you switch:

- **Run a degree audit** (most schools have an online tool or an advisor who can do it) to see which of your credits count toward the new major.
- **Ask how many semesters you would add.** Switching between related majors (for example, biology to chemistry, or marketing to management) often costs less than switching between unrelated ones.
- **Check financial aid rules.** Aid programs have limits on how long you can receive aid. Ask your financial aid office how a switch affects your aid.
- **Check entry requirements.** Some majors have a minimum GPA, prerequisite courses or a separate application.

The earlier you switch, the less it usually costs, because fewer of your credits are tied to the old major.

## A step-by-step plan

**1. Name the problem.** Write down what is not working: the subject, the careers, the workload or something outside your major entirely.

**2. Explore without committing.** Take an intro course in the major you are considering, or audit one. The [College Major Quiz](/college-major-quiz) can suggest majors that fit your interests if you are unsure where to go.

**3. Look at the careers at the end.** For your current and new major, list the careers they lead to and compare pay, training and daily work. Our guide on [what major should I choose](/guides/what-major-should-i-choose) shows which majors fit each interest type.

**4. Talk to people.** Meet your academic advisor, a professor in the new department, and if you can, someone who works in a career the new major leads to.

**5. Run the numbers.** Get the degree audit, the extra semesters and the aid impact in writing.

**6. Decide by a date.** Set a deadline, such as the end of the current semester, so the decision does not drift.

## Questions to ask your advisor

Bring a short list to your meeting so you leave with answers, not just reassurance:

- Which of my completed credits count toward the new major, and which become electives?
- How many semesters would the switch add if I take a normal course load?
- Are there prerequisites I must finish before I can be admitted, and is there a GPA requirement?
- Can I take any of the new major's required courses next semester, before I officially switch?
- Is there a related major or a minor that gets me most of the way with fewer extra credits?
- Who in the new department can I talk to about what graduates go on to do?

Write the answers down. They turn a vague worry into a plan you can compare side by side.

## If you decide to stay

Choosing to stay is also a valid outcome. You can still shape your path:

- add a minor in the area that excites you;
- choose electives and projects that point toward the careers you want;
- build skills that transfer to many careers, such as problem solving and coordination (see our [transferable skills list](/guides/transferable-skills-list)).

## Common questions

### Is it bad to change majors?

No. About 30% of students change at least once within three years. What employers look at is your degree, your skills and your experience, not how many times you changed course on the way.

### Is it too late to change my major in junior year?

It is not too late, but it usually costs more time. Get a degree audit first and look for a related major or a minor that uses your existing credits.

### Will changing my major delay graduation?

It depends on how many credits transfer. Switching early and between related fields keeps the delay small. Ask an advisor for a semester-by-semester plan before you decide.
