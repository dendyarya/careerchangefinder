"Best career to switch to" lists usually pick jobs that sound good. This one is built from data. For each of the 875 U.S. careers with full O\*NET and BLS data, we ranked the 25 careers whose skills, knowledge, work activities and interests match it most closely. Then we counted how often each career shows up in those lists.

A career that appears in many lists is one that people from many different backgrounds are already well prepared for. That makes it a realistic switch, not just an attractive one.

## How to read the tables

- **Strong match for…** is the number of the 875 careers whose 25 best matches include this one.
- **Median pay** is the BLS national median for May 2025. Half of workers earn more, half less.
- **Preparation** is the O\*NET Job Zone: 2 means some preparation (often a high school diploma and on-the-job training), 3 a vocational certificate or associate degree, 4 usually a bachelor's degree.
- Careers that share a broader BLS pay figure with other jobs are left out, so every pay number below is the career's own.
- Every career here pays more than the median for all U.S. workers, $50,980 (BLS, May 2025).

**What stands out:** production, energy and rail jobs dominate. Most of the jobs that list them as a strong match are production, transportation, construction and repair jobs: for example, 42 of the 93 careers that match refinery operators are production jobs, and 46 of the 90 that match riggers are construction jobs. If you work with machines or on a crew, these are often a short step away. Petroleum refinery operators pay nearly twice the U.S. median and usually need some preparation rather than a degree.

Two careers in this table work differently. Administrative services managers usually reach the role after years of office experience, and LPNs need a state-approved practical nursing program, which typically takes about 1 year, plus a license exam ([BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/licensed-practical-and-licensed-vocational-nurses.htm)).

For more options at this level, see [jobs without a degree](/careers/education/no-degree) and [career change to skilled trades](/career-change/to-skilled-trades).

## Best switches at the bachelor's degree level

These careers usually need a bachelor's degree (Job Zone 4). They are strong matches for many professional and office jobs, so people who already have a degree, or who are in a related role, can often move across.

**What stands out:** management analysis and project management are the most open doors. Both reward problem solving, coordination and judgment, which are important in hundreds of careers, and neither is tied to one industry. Management analysts are a strong match for managers (30 careers), computer workers (13), scientists (10), business and finance specialists (10) and educators (8).

The manager roles in this list (financial, sales, marketing, purchasing, HR, compensation and benefits) pay the most, but they usually go to people who already have experience in that function. The realistic path is often two steps: first into the specialist role (for example, HR specialist or buyer), then into management.

Medical dosimetrists plan radiation doses for cancer treatment. They are a strong match for many science and health careers, but the field is small: BLS counts 3,410 jobs nationwide, and a professional certification is often expected.

## How to use this list

**1. Start from your own job, not from the list.** A career that suits 80 other jobs may not suit yours. Look up your current job in the [Career Change Tool](/career-change/tool) to see your personal top 25, with the pay change and extra education for each.

**2. Check the hard parts.** Every career profile lists the downsides O\*NET work context data shows, such as outdoor work in all weather, tight deadlines or exposure to hazards. Many of the best-paid hands-on jobs in the first table involve shift work and physical conditions that do not suit everyone.

**3. Check pay where you live.** National medians hide big differences between states. Every career profile links to its pay in each state.

**4. Look at the number of jobs.** Some strong matches are small fields. A career with a few thousand jobs nationwide may have few openings near you.

**5. Look at your age group.** If you are changing careers later in life, our pages for [career change at 40](/career-change/at-40) and [career change at 50](/career-change/at-50) list careers that pay well and already have a large share of older workers.

## Common questions

### What is the easiest career to switch to?

The easiest switch is the one closest to your current job. Across all 875 careers, the closest match scores 86 out of 99 for a typical job, and 88.2% of each job's 25 best matches usually need no more preparation. Look up your own job in the [Career Change Tool](/career-change/tool).

### Which careers are best to switch to without a degree?

In our data, refinery operators, locomotive engineers, rail-track equipment operators and riggers pay well above the U.S. median and are strong matches for many hands-on jobs. LPNs and administrative services managers are options at the certificate level. See the first table above.

### Which careers pay the most after a switch?

Among careers that match many other jobs, financial managers ($166,570), marketing managers ($166,790) and HR managers ($149,280) have the highest median pay, but they usually require experience in that function first.

## Method in brief

Match scores compare O\*NET importance ratings for transferable skills (35%), knowledge (25%), work activities (15%) and interests (15%), add up to 10 points when O\*NET lists the careers as related, and subtract points for much more preparation or a big pay drop. Each of the 875 careers gets a list of its 25 best matches; the counts above are how many of those lists include each career. The full rules are on our [Methodology](/methodology) page.
