The fear of starting over at the bottom keeps many people in jobs they have outgrown. But a career change does not have to mean a pay cut. When you move to a career that uses the skills you already have, you can often keep your pay or raise it.

This guide shows how often that is possible, which kinds of moves protect your income, and the steps to take so the new job pays at least what the old one did.

## How often can you switch without losing pay?

We studied every U.S. career that has its own BLS pay figure, 732 in total. For each one, we looked at its 25 closest career matches by skills, knowledge and daily work, and asked a simple question: does at least one of them pay at least 10% more at the median, usually need no more preparation, and not depend on a promotion to manager?

The answer was yes for **648 of the 732 careers, or 88.5%**. Those careers employ 87.1% of the workers in the study. For careers with such a move, the closest one paid a median of **24.4% more**. You can read the full method and results in our [better-paying careers study](/research/better-paying-careers).

Not every career has such a move. General and operations managers, for example, already earn a median of $105,770, and none of their close matches that need no more preparation pays 10% more, other than manager roles that usually require a promotion. If you already earn near the top of your field, protecting pay is the realistic goal rather than raising it.

## Where you live matters

Pay gaps between careers are not the same in every state. When we repeated the study with state pay figures, the share of workers in careers with a better-paying close match ranged from 92% in West Virginia to 67.8% in the District of Columbia. Check the state numbers on any career's salary page before you decide, because a move that pays more nationally may not pay more where you live.

## 6 steps to change careers without a pay cut

### 1. Know your real number

Write down your total pay: base salary, overtime, bonuses and benefits such as retirement matching and health insurance. A new job with the same salary but no retirement match is a pay cut.

### 2. Start from close matches, not dream jobs

The closer the new career is to your current skills, the more of your experience counts, and the more an employer will pay for it. Use the [career change tool](/career-change/tool) to list the 25 closest careers to your job, then sort them by pay change.

### 3. Filter out moves that need years of new schooling

A higher median does not help if it takes four years of school to reach it. On each career page, check the preparation level. Moves that need no more preparation than you have now let you switch without a gap in income.

### 4. Compare entry pay, not just the median

A median is the middle of the range, not what a new hire earns. Look at the lower end of the pay range, the 10th or 25th percentile, on the career's salary page. If that number is close to what you earn now, a switch is low risk.

### 5. Bring your experience into the negotiation

Career changers often accept the first offer because they feel lucky to be hired. Do not. List the results from your past job that matter in the new one, and use them to ask for a salary above the entry level.

### 6. Consider a bridge role

If the career you want pays more but needs experience you do not have, look for a bridge: a role in the same organization or industry that uses your current skills while you learn the new ones. Moving inside a company often protects your pay better than starting fresh elsewhere.

## When a short-term pay cut can be worth it

Sometimes a step back makes sense. A pay cut can pay off when:

- the new career's median is much higher than your current pay, and the path to it is short;
- the new field has steady demand, so you are unlikely to get stuck at entry level;
- you can afford the gap without debt.

Do the math before you decide: how long will it take for the higher pay to make up for the months or years of lower pay?

## A worked example

Take a customer service representative earning the national median of $44,770. The closest better-paying match is sales representative of services, a 90% skill match with a median of $69,990.

- **Is the median gain real?** Yes: $25,220 a year, or 56.3%.
- **What does a new hire risk?** The 25th percentile for sales representatives of services is $47,670, still above the customer service median. The 10th percentile is $37,980, below it. So a weak first offer could mean a short-term dip.
- **How to protect pay:** apply where your service record counts, such as companies whose products you already support, and use your customer results in the negotiation. Many sales roles also pay commission on top of base salary, so ask how pay is split and what typical first-year earnings are.
- **Preparation needed:** both careers are in Job Zone 2, so no new degree is required.

The same check works for any move: compare your pay with the new career's 25th percentile and median, then decide how much risk you can take.

## Common questions

### Can I change careers and earn more?

Usually, yes. For 88.5% of U.S. careers with their own BLS pay figure, at least one of the 25 closest matches pays at least 10% more at the median and usually needs no more preparation. See the [study](/research/better-paying-careers) for details.

### What careers pay more with the same skills?

It depends on your current job. For cashiers, tellers pay 30.9% more; for customer service representatives, sales representatives of services pay 56.3% more. Enter your job in the [career change tool](/career-change/tool) to see yours.

### Do career changers earn less at first?

Often a little, because employers pay for proven experience in the role. Choosing a close match, where most of your experience counts, is the best way to keep the dip small or avoid it.

### Is it worth taking a pay cut for a career I like more?

It can be, if you can afford it and the long-term pay is good. Compare the new career's median and top pay with your current path, and decide how many years of lower pay you can accept.
