If you are asking whether it is too late to change careers, you are probably over 35, and you probably feel like everyone else made up their mind years ago. The data says otherwise. Older workers are a large part of the U.S. workforce, people keep starting new jobs well into their fifties, and most career changes do not require going back to school.

This guide looks at the numbers behind those statements and then at the factors that really decide whether a change makes sense for you.

## The short answer

For most people, it is not too late. In 2025, the median age of a U.S. worker was 42.1, and 43% of workers were 45 or older ([BLS Current Population Survey](https://www.bls.gov/cps/cpsaat11b.htm), 2025 annual averages). Employers hire people in their forties and fifties every day, because those people are most of the workforce.

What changes with age is not whether you can switch, but which switches make sense. A move that needs four years of school is a harder sell at 52 than at 28. A move that uses skills you already have, with a few months of training, works at almost any age.

## How old are U.S. workers?

The Current Population Survey counts workers by age band. In 2025:

| Age group | Share of U.S. workers |
|---|---|
| 45 and older | 43% |
| 55 and older | 23.2% |
| Median age | 42.1 years |

Source: BLS Current Population Survey, 2025 annual averages (11 months; October was not collected).

In careers like these, a new hire in their late forties is not unusual. They look like most of the people already doing the job. Our pages on [changing careers at 40](/career-change/at-40) and [at 50](/career-change/at-50) list many more.

## People keep changing jobs throughout their working lives

The Bureau of Labor Statistics has followed one group of Americans, born from 1957 to 1964, since they were teenagers. By age 58 they had held an average of 12.9 jobs ([BLS National Longitudinal Survey of Youth 1979](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/nlsoy.nr0.htm)). Job changes slow down with age, but they do not stop:

- from ages 35 to 44, people in this group started an average of 2.9 jobs;
- from 45 to 54, an average of 2.2 jobs;
- from 55 to 58, an average of 1.3 jobs.

Not every new job is a new career, but the pattern is clear: starting over in your forties and fifties is a normal part of working life, not an exception.

## Most career changes do not need a new degree

The fear behind "too late" is often the fear of years of school. For most moves, that fear is not justified. On this site, every career is matched with the 25 careers that use the most similar skills, knowledge and daily work. Across all 875 careers with full data, **88.2% of those close matches usually need no more preparation** than the job the person already has, based on the O\*NET Job Zones.

Pay can rise too. In our [better-paying careers study](/research/better-paying-careers), 88.5% of U.S. careers with their own BLS pay figure had at least one close match that pays at least 10% more at the median and usually needs no extra schooling. For those careers, the typical gap to the closest such match was 24.4%.

Training time is usually measured in months, not years. O\*NET asks workers how much on-the-job training their job takes. For 267 of the 875 careers, the most common answer is a month or less, and for another 190 it is one to three months.

## What really decides whether it is too late

Age matters less than these five things. Check each one honestly.

1. **How long the training takes compared with how long you plan to work.** If you plan to work another 15 years, a one-year certificate is a small part of that time. A seven-year path to a medical license is not.
2. **How much income you can give up while you switch.** Moves to careers that need no new schooling often involve no gap at all. Moves that need a degree can cost several years of reduced pay.
3. **How physical the work is.** Some roles, such as roofing or long-distance trucking, are harder to start later in life. Others, such as dispatching, inspection or bookkeeping, are not.
4. **Whether licensing has an age or time limit.** A few fields, such as commercial aviation or some police departments, have age limits for new hires. Most careers have none.
5. **How well your experience transfers.** Skills like judgment, coordination and time management carry into most jobs. The more of them a new career uses, the faster you will be productive.

## Careers that are easier to start later

Careers tend to suit a later start when they:

- reward judgment and people skills more than physical speed;
- train new hires on the job or through short programs;
- have many workers in their forties and fifties already;
- have steady demand across many employers.

Good places to look include office management, inspection and compliance work, healthcare support roles, transportation dispatch, and teaching or training roles where your past career becomes your subject. ## How to make a late career change work

### Start from what you already know

Pick a new career that sits close to your current one. If you are an accountant, a move into financial analysis or compliance is shorter than a move into nursing. Our [jobs similar to](/jobs-similar-to) pages list the closest careers for every job.

### Test before you commit

Before quitting, find a low-cost way to try the work: volunteer, take one evening course, shadow someone for a day, or do a small freelance project. A month of real experience tells you more than a year of reading.

### Use your age as an advantage

Employers value reliability, judgment and the ability to handle difficult people. Say so directly in your resume and interviews. Our guide to [writing a career change resume](/guides/career-change-resume) shows how to lead with transferable results.

### Plan the money

Work out how long you can live on reduced income, and choose a training path that fits that time. If the numbers do not work for a degree, choose a closer move first and build from there.

## Common questions

### Is 40 too old to change careers?

No. In 2025 the median age of a U.S. worker was 42.1, so someone switching at 40 is younger than half of the workforce. Most moves to similar careers need no new degree. See [career change at 40](/career-change/at-40) for options with pay and training time.

### Is 50 too old to change careers?

Not for most careers. 23.2% of U.S. workers are 55 or older, and many careers have a majority of workers over 45. Focus on moves that use your current skills and need months, not years, of training. See [career change at 50](/career-change/at-50).

### Will I have to take a pay cut?

Not necessarily. For 88.5% of careers with their own BLS pay figure, at least one close match pays 10% or more above the current median with no extra schooling. Read [how to change careers without taking a pay cut](/guides/how-to-change-careers-without-a-pay-cut).

### What is the best career to switch to later in life?

The best one uses the skills you already have. Start with the [career change tool](/career-change/tool), enter your current job, and look at matches that need no more preparation and pay at least as much.
