Whether you stepped away to raise children, care for a family member, recover from illness, study or travel, coming back to work can feel harder than any career change. Your skills may feel rusty, your network may have moved on, and the gap on your resume can seem like the first thing anyone will notice.

The good news: career breaks are common, employers see them all the time, and many careers are built to train new people quickly. This guide walks through how to come back, whether to your old field or a new one.

## First, decide: same field or new direction?

A career break is a natural moment to reconsider. Ask yourself:

- Do I still want the work I did before, or did I leave partly because it was not right?
- Has my field changed a lot while I was away, through new technology, rules or licensing?
- Do I need a different schedule now, such as part time, remote or no night shifts?

If you want to return to your old field, focus on refreshing your skills and credentials. If you want a new direction, start with careers close to your past experience, which are the fastest to enter. The [career change tool](/career-change/tool) shows them for any job.

## Careers that welcome returners

Some careers are easier to re-enter because they train new hires or do not expect recent experience in the field. O\*NET asks workers how much related experience a new hire needs. For 257 of the 875 careers with full data on this site, the most common answer is **none**.

O\*NET also asks how long on-the-job training takes. For 457 of the 875 careers, the most common answer is three months or less.

Careers with both features include many roles in office administration, healthcare support, customer service, sales, transportation and public service. Our list of [careers by education level](/careers/education/no-degree) and the pages on careers for people who prefer [low-stress](/careers/personality/low-stress) or [hands-on](/careers/personality/hands-on) work are good places to browse.

## How to explain the gap

You do not need to apologize or give details. Be brief, honest and forward-looking.

**On your resume:** add a single line for the gap with dates and a short description:

> 2021–2025: Career break for full-time caregiving. Managed household budget and medical appointments for a family member; completed online course in bookkeeping.

**In interviews:** use one or two sentences, then move to what you are ready to do now:

> "I took four years off to care for my children. Now that they're in school, I'm ready to return full time, and I've spent the last few months refreshing my skills with a course in [skill]."

Things to avoid:

- long explanations or personal details;
- sounding apologetic;
- hiding the gap, which raises more questions than it answers.

## Refresh your skills before you apply

Choose two or three updates that matter most for your target job:

- **Software and tools.** Office software, scheduling systems, industry tools. Short online courses are often enough.
- **Licenses and certifications.** Check whether your license lapsed and what renewal requires. Some fields, such as nursing and teaching, offer refresher or re-entry programs.
- **Industry changes.** Read recent job ads to see what is new in the field. Requirements that appear often are the ones to learn.

## Count what you did during the break

Many activities during a career break build skills employers value:

- running a household budget (managing money);
- organizing schedules, appointments and logistics (time management, coordination);
- volunteering at school, church or community groups (teamwork, leadership);
- caregiving (patience, judgment, working with medical systems);
- courses, freelance work or side projects.

Do not list everything. Choose the activities that connect to your target job and describe them with results.

## Rebuild your network

- Contact former colleagues and managers; many are glad to hear from you and may know of openings.
- Join a professional association or local group in your field.
- Update your LinkedIn profile; see [how to explain a career change on LinkedIn](/guides/career-change-linkedin).
- Ask for short conversations with people in your target role; see our guide to [informational interviews](/guides/informational-interviews).

## Ways back in

- **Returnship programs:** some large employers run paid programs for people returning after a break, often lasting a few months.
- **Part-time or contract work:** a lower-commitment role rebuilds confidence and recent experience.
- **Temporary staffing agencies:** a fast way to get recent experience on your resume.
- **Volunteering** in your target field, which adds references and current examples.

## A 90-day return plan

| Days | Focus | Actions |
|---|---|---|
| 1–30 | Decide and refresh | Choose your target role, list the two or three skills to update, start one short course, update your resume |
| 31–60 | Reconnect | Contact 10 former colleagues, update LinkedIn, have two or three informational conversations, apply to returnship or part-time roles |
| 61–90 | Apply and interview | Send targeted applications each week, practice your answer about the gap, follow up on every lead |

Track what you do each week. Progress can feel slow at first, but each conversation and application builds momentum. If nothing moves after 90 days, revisit your target: a role closer to your past experience, or one that trains new hires, may be a faster first step.

## If your old license or certification lapsed

Many licensed fields have a re-entry path:

- **Nursing:** most states offer refresher courses for nurses returning after several years.
- **Teaching:** states usually allow reinstatement after continuing education or a short course.
- **Trades and technical fields:** check with the licensing board; some only require continuing education hours.

Call the licensing body before you spend money on courses, so you know exactly what is required.

## Common questions

### How do I explain a 5-year gap on my resume?

Add one line with the dates and a short reason, such as caregiving or health, plus anything relevant you did during that time. Keep it brief and focus the rest of the resume on what you can do now.

### Is it harder to get hired after a career break?

It can take longer, mainly because your network and recent examples are thinner. Refreshing one or two key skills, reconnecting with former colleagues and starting with careers that train new hires all shorten the search.

### What are good jobs to return to work after a break?

Look for careers that need no related experience and short on-the-job training. In O\*NET, 257 of 875 careers usually need no related experience, and 457 have training of three months or less.

### Should I return to my old career or start a new one?

If you still enjoy the work and your credentials are current or easy to renew, returning is usually fastest. If not, choose a career close to your past experience. The [career change tool](/career-change/tool) shows the closest options.
