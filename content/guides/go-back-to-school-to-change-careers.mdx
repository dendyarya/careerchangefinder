Going back to school is the first idea many people have when they want a new career. Sometimes it is the right one: you cannot become a nurse, an engineer or a lawyer without the degree. But for most career changes, school is not required, and starting with it can cost years and thousands of dollars you did not need to spend.

This guide helps you decide whether school is necessary for your move, whether it is likely to pay off, and what to do instead when it is not.

## Most career changes do not need a new degree

Every career on this site is matched with the 25 careers that use the most similar skills, knowledge and daily work. Across the 875 careers with full data, **88.2% of those close matches usually need no more preparation** than the current job, based on the O\*NET Job Zones. The pattern holds at every level:

| Your current career's preparation | Careers | Typical number of the 25 closest matches that need no more preparation |
|---|---|---|
| Job Zone 2: some preparation | 322 | 24 |
| Job Zone 3: medium preparation | 194 | 21 |
| Job Zone 4: considerable preparation (bachelor's) | 213 | 24 |
| Job Zone 5: extensive preparation (graduate) | 146 | 25 |

Source: O\*NET 31.0; our calculation (median across careers in each zone).

In other words, if you want to move to a career that is similar to your current one, you can usually do it with the education you have. School becomes necessary when you want to move to a career that is far from your current skills, or one that requires a specific license.

## Education still affects pay

That does not mean education does not matter. Across the U.S. workforce, earnings rise and unemployment falls with each level of education:

| Education | Median weekly earnings | Unemployment rate |
|---|---|---|
| Doctoral degree | $2,307 | 1.8% |
| Professional degree | $2,294 | 1.9% |
| Master's degree | $1,876 | 2.6% |
| Bachelor's degree | $1,578 | 2.8% |
| Associate's degree | $1,135 | 3.0% |
| Some college, no degree | $1,062 | 3.8% |
| High school diploma | $966 | 4.3% |
| Less than a high school diploma | $770 | 6.1% |

Source: [BLS, Education pays, 2025](https://www.bls.gov/careeroutlook/2026/data-on-display/education-pays.htm). Full-time wage and salary workers age 25 and older; 11-month averages because October 2025 data were not collected.

These are averages across everyone, including people who chose their field at 18. For a career changer, the useful question is narrower: does the specific career you want require the degree, and will the higher pay cover the cost and the years in school?

## When going back to school makes sense

School is usually worth it when most of these are true:

1. **The career legally requires it.** Nursing, teaching in public schools, counseling, accounting (for CPAs), law, medicine and most engineering jobs need a specific degree or license.
2. **The pay gain is large and lasting.** Compare the new career's median pay with yours. A gain of a few thousand dollars may never repay the cost of a degree; a gain of $20,000 a year or more often will.
3. **You have tested the work.** You have volunteered, shadowed, or worked near the field and know you like it.
4. **You can afford the time.** You have savings, a partner's income, employer support or a part-time program that lets you keep working.
5. **The program is short or credits transfer.** Accelerated programs for people who already have a bachelor's degree, such as second-degree nursing programs, can cut the time sharply.

## When it usually does not

School is often the wrong first step when:

- a close career match needs no more preparation than you already have;
- employers in the new field hire based on skills, portfolios or certifications rather than degrees, which is common in sales, many trades, and parts of tech and design;
- the main thing you lack is experience, not knowledge;
- the debt would be large compared with the pay gain.

## Cheaper and faster alternatives

| Option | Typical length | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Short course or online class | Weeks | Testing a field, learning one tool |
| Professional certificate | A few months to a year | Healthcare support, IT support, project management, trades |
| Apprenticeship | 1 to 5 years, paid | Skilled trades, some tech and healthcare roles |
| Associate degree | About 2 years | Dental hygiene, radiologic and respiratory therapy, many technician roles |
| Employer-paid training | Varies | Moving within a company you already work for |

Certificates and associate degrees can lead to good pay. On this site, the 26 careers with their own BLS pay figure where an associate degree is the most common education have a median of $70,340, above the U.S. median for all workers of $50,980. Examples include [dental hygienists](/careers/dental-hygienists) ($98,100) and [radiation therapists](/careers/radiation-therapists) ($105,310).

## How to decide: a 4-step check

1. **List your top three target careers.** Use the [career change tool](/career-change/tool) to see how close each is to your current job.
2. **Check what each one requires.** Each career profile shows the education most workers have and the preparation level. Note any license.
3. **Estimate the payoff.** Subtract your current pay from the new career's median, then divide the total cost of school (tuition plus lost income) by that yearly gain. The result is roughly how many years it takes to break even.
4. **Try it before you enroll.** Take one course or volunteer for a month. If you still want it, enroll.

## A worked example: is the degree worth it?

Suppose a retail salesperson earning the national median of $35,410 is considering a two-year dental hygiene program, which leads to a career with a median of $98,100.

- **Yearly pay gain once working:** about $62,690 at the median.
- **Cost of the program:** tuition for two years at a public two-year college, at about $4,000 a year, is roughly $8,000. Add the income given up if you study full time, up to about $70,820 over two years, and the total is near $79,000.
- **Years to break even:** $79,000 divided by $62,690 is about 1.3 years after starting work.

Dental hygiene programs often cost more than general community college tuition because of clinical training, so check the real price. Even at double the cost, the payoff in this example is fast. Run the same numbers for your own target; when the yearly gain is small, the break-even point can stretch past ten years.

## Common questions

### Is it worth going back to school at 40?

It can be, if the career requires a degree and pays clearly more. Many adults finish programs in their forties. If a close career match needs no new degree, that is usually a faster and cheaper option.

### Do I need a degree to change careers?

Usually not. 88.2% of the 25 closest matches for each career on this site need no more preparation than the current job. You need a degree mainly when the new career legally requires one.

### What is the fastest degree for a career change?

For people with a bachelor's degree, accelerated or second-degree programs, such as accelerated nursing programs, are often the fastest. For others, a two-year associate degree in a healthcare or technical field can lead to good pay.

### Are certificates worth it?

For many careers, yes. Certificates in healthcare support, IT, project management and the trades are often what employers ask for, and they take months rather than years.
