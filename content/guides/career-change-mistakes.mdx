Most failed career changes do not fail because the person was not capable. They fail because of a few avoidable mistakes made early: the wrong target, the wrong order, or no plan for money and time. This guide covers the ten most common ones and what to do instead.

## 1. Choosing a career only by its reputation

Careers that sound exciting, such as UX design, data science or nursing, get the most attention. Popularity says little about whether you will like the daily work or whether you can get in quickly.

**Instead:** start from what you are already good at and what interests you. The [career change quiz](/career-change/quiz) and the [career change tool](/career-change/tool) suggest careers that fit you, not just careers that are popular.

## 2. Jumping to the most distant option first

A teacher who wants to become a software engineer faces years of new learning. A teacher who moves into corporate training or instructional design uses most of their existing skills from day one.

**Instead:** look at close matches first. Across all careers on this site, 88.2% of each job's 25 closest matches usually need no more preparation than the current job. A close move is faster, cheaper and less risky, and you can always take a second step later.

## 3. Going back to school before testing the field

Many people sign up for a degree program before they have done a single day of the new work. Some discover halfway through that they do not like it.

**Instead:** test first. Take one short course, volunteer, shadow someone or do a small paid project. Spend money on a degree only once you are sure. Read [should you go back to school to change careers?](/guides/go-back-to-school-to-change-careers)

## 4. Ignoring pay until the offer arrives

Some career changers assume they must take a large pay cut and accept whatever they are offered. Others assume pay will be higher and are disappointed.

**Instead:** check pay early. Every career profile on this site shows BLS median pay and the pay range. In our [better-paying careers study](/research/better-paying-careers), 88.5% of careers with their own pay figure had a close match that pays at least 10% more. Read [how to change careers without a pay cut](/guides/how-to-change-careers-without-a-pay-cut).

## 5. Underestimating your transferable skills

People often describe their past as "irrelevant" in interviews. That throws away the strongest part of their application.

**Instead:** name your transferable skills and back them with results. O\*NET rates judgment and decision making at 3 or higher on a 5-point scale in 672 of 875 careers, and time management in 648. Our [transferable skills list](/guides/transferable-skills-list) helps you find yours.

## 6. Quitting without a money plan

Leaving a job before you know how long the switch will take is the fastest way to end up back where you started, or somewhere worse.

**Instead:** estimate the timeline (see [how long a career change takes](/guides/how-long-does-a-career-change-take)), then make sure you can cover your costs for that period plus a buffer. Keep your current job while you test and train if you can.

## 7. Applying without changing your resume

Sending your old resume to a new field rarely works. Recruiters spend seconds on each one and look for the new job's keywords and tasks.

**Instead:** rewrite your resume for the new career. Lead with a short summary of the switch, then list results that match the new job's main tasks. See the [career change resume guide](/guides/career-change-resume) and the [cover letter guide](/guides/career-change-cover-letter).

## 8. Relying only on job boards

Career changers are rarely the first choice of an automated filter, because their past titles do not match.

**Instead:** talk to people. Informational interviews, professional groups and former colleagues lead to openings and referrals where a human reads your story. See our guide to [informational interviews](/guides/informational-interviews).

## 9. Not having a clear answer to "Why the change?"

Every interviewer will ask why you are switching. A vague or negative answer ("I was burned out") raises doubts.

**Instead:** prepare a short, positive answer that connects your past to the new role. Our guide on [how to answer "Why are you changing careers?"](/guides/why-are-you-changing-careers) has examples.

## 10. Waiting for certainty

No career change comes with a guarantee. People who wait until they are completely sure often wait for years.

**Instead:** aim to be fairly sure, then take a small step that can be reversed: one course, one conversation, one application. Each step gives you more information than another month of thinking.

## A quick checklist before you switch

- I know which parts of my current work I want to keep and which I want to leave.
- I have compared at least three careers by pay, preparation and daily tasks.
- I have tested my top choice in a small way.
- I know how long the switch will take and can afford that time.
- My resume and LinkedIn describe the new direction.
- I can explain my switch in two or three positive sentences.

## Mistakes after you switch

The first months in a new career have their own traps:

- **Hiding what you do not know.** Ask questions early. New people are expected to learn; pretending slows you down.
- **Comparing yourself with people who have years in the field.** Compare yourself with where you were a month ago.
- **Dropping your old network.** Former colleagues are often your best references and future clients or partners.
- **Ignoring early warning signs.** If the work is clearly wrong after three to six months, talk to your manager or look at a nearby role rather than waiting years.
- **Stopping learning once you are hired.** The first year is when skills build fastest. Keep a short list of what to learn next.

Give a new career at least six months before judging it. Most people find the first three months the hardest.

## Common questions

### What is the biggest career change mistake?

Choosing a distant career without testing it. It costs the most time and money if it turns out to be the wrong fit. Start with a close match or a small trial first.

### Should I quit my job before changing careers?

Usually not. Most career changes can be researched, tested and even trained for while you work. Quit when you have an offer or a clear plan you can afford.

### Is it a mistake to take a pay cut to change careers?

Not always. It can be worth it when the long-term pay is higher and the path is short. Do the math first: how many years will it take for the new career to make up the difference?

### How do I avoid regret after a career change?

Test the new work before committing, choose a field close to your strengths, and keep contacts in your old field. If it does not work out, you can return or take another step.
