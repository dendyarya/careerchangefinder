A career change resume has one job: to show a hiring manager in a new field that you can already do much of the work. A standard resume lists what you did. A career change resume translates it into what the new job needs, in the new job's words.

This guide shows how, step by step, with a worked example of a teacher moving into corporate training.

## The short version

- Use a **combination (hybrid) format**: a short summary and a skills section at the top, then your work history.
- Write a **3-line summary** that names the target role and the strongest overlap.
- Build the **skills section from the target job**, not your current one.
- Rewrite each **bullet as an action with a result,** in the new field's vocabulary.
- Put **new training or certificates** near the top, not at the bottom.
- Cut what the new employer does not need, and keep it to **one page** if you can, two at most.

## Step 1: Know exactly what the new job needs

You cannot translate your experience until you know the target. For each role you apply to:

1. **Read three to five job postings** for the same title and list the skills and tools they all mention.
2. **Check the O\*NET skill ratings** for that career. Every career profile on this site shows the skills that matter most, rated from 1 to 5. They tell you what employers in that field value across the country, not just in one posting.
3. **Compare with your own job** in the [Transferable Skills Finder](/transferable-skills). The skills that are high in both jobs are your selling points. The ones that are higher in the new job are the gaps to address.

**Example:** for [Training and Development Specialists](/careers/training-and-development-specialists), O\*NET rates instructing at 4.38, social perceptiveness at 4.00, judgment and decision making at 3.88 and coordination at 3.75. A teacher's job already rates high on all four, so those four skills lead the resume.

## Step 2: Choose a combination format

There are three common resume formats:

| Format | What comes first | Good for a career change? |
|---|---|---|
| Chronological | Work history, newest first | Weak: your job titles point to the old field |
| Functional | Skills only, little or no history | Risky: many recruiters distrust a resume without dates |
| Combination (hybrid) | Summary and skills, then work history | Best: leads with what transfers, still shows a clear record |

The combination format lets the reader see your fit for the new role in the first few seconds, then confirms it with your real history.

## Step 3: Write a summary that explains the switch

Your summary sits right under your name. In two or three sentences it should say what you are aiming for, why you fit, and what you have already done to prepare.

**Weak:**
> Dedicated teacher with 9 years of experience seeking new opportunities.

**Strong:**
> Educator with 9 years of experience designing and delivering lessons for groups of 25 to 30 learners, now moving into corporate training. Skilled in needs assessment, curriculum design and measuring learning results. Completed a certificate in instructional design (2026).

The strong version names the target, uses the new field's terms (needs assessment, curriculum design) and shows proof of preparation.

## Step 4: Build the skills section from the target job

List six to ten skills, starting with the ones the target job rates highest and that you can prove. Use the employer's words.

For the teacher-to-trainer example:

- Training design and delivery (instructing)
- Needs assessment and learner analysis (social perceptiveness)
- Program evaluation and reporting (judgment and decision making)
- Scheduling and stakeholder coordination (coordination)
- Learning management systems: name the ones you used
- Presentation and facilitation for adults

Add technical tools the postings mention only if you can use them. A short course is enough to list a tool honestly; a single tutorial is not.

## Step 5: Rewrite your bullets as results in the new field's words

Each bullet should start with an action verb, describe something the new job also does, and end with a result. A simple formula: **action + what + for whom + result**.

| Before (teacher wording) | After (training wording) |
|---|---|
| Taught 5th grade math and science | Designed and delivered 180 days of math and science instruction for 28 learners a year |
| Held parent-teacher conferences | Ran 60+ stakeholder meetings a year to report progress and agree on next steps |
| Made tests and graded them | Built assessments and used the results to adjust content, raising unit test scores |
| Mentored new teachers | Coached 3 new staff through their first year, including lesson observations and feedback |

The numbers above are illustrations. Use your own, and only claim results you can explain in an interview.

The same method works in any field:

- **Retail to customer service or banking:** "Handled 100+ transactions a shift with a balanced cash drawer" shows the accuracy that [Tellers](/careers/tellers) need.
- **Nursing to clinical research:** "Documented care for 5 to 6 patients per shift under hospital protocols" shows the careful record-keeping clinical research coordinators rely on.
- **Military to operations:** describe the size of the team, budget or equipment you were responsible for in civilian terms.

## Step 6: Put new training near the top

If you have taken a course, earned a certificate or finished a project for the new field, do not hide it at the bottom. Add a short "Training and certifications" or "Relevant projects" section right after the skills, with dates. It answers the question every reader has: has this person already started?

If the new role usually needs more preparation than your current one, every career profile on this site shows the most common education level and any certification that is often expected. Use it to decide what to complete before you apply.

## Step 7: Cut what does not help

A career change resume is a selection, not a record of everything:

- Drop duties that only matter in the old field (for example, a list of curriculum standards).
- Shorten jobs older than 10 to 15 years to one line, unless they are relevant to the new role.
- Remove jargon the new field will not recognize, or replace it with plain words.

## Step 8: Get past applicant tracking systems

Many employers screen resumes with software before a person reads them. Keep the file easy to read:

- Use standard headings: Summary, Skills, Experience, Education, Certifications.
- Avoid tables, text boxes and images for important text.
- Use the exact job title and key terms from the posting where they truthfully describe you.
- Save as a PDF or Word file, whichever the posting asks for.

## A full example

**Jordan Lee** (fictional example)
Corporate Trainer · city, state · email · LinkedIn

**Summary**
Educator with 9 years of experience designing and delivering instruction for groups of 25 to 30 learners, now moving into corporate training. Skilled in needs assessment, curriculum design and measuring learning results. Certificate in instructional design, 2026.

**Skills**
Training design and delivery · Needs assessment · Program evaluation · Stakeholder communication · Facilitation for adults · Learning management systems

**Training and certifications**
Instructional design certificate, 2026 · Adult learning course, 2026

**Experience**
5th Grade Teacher, school name, 2017–2026
- Designed and delivered 180 days of math and science instruction a year for 28 learners with different needs.
- Built assessments and used the results to adjust content each unit.
- Ran 60+ stakeholder meetings a year to report progress and agree on action plans.
- Coached 3 new staff through their first year.

**Education**
Bachelor's degree, field, university

## Before you send it

- Read each bullet and ask: would a manager in the new field care about this?
- Check that your summary names the target role.
- Pair the resume with a [career change cover letter](/guides/career-change-cover-letter) that explains the switch in a few lines.
