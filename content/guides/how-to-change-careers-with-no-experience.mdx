"No experience" is the most common worry people have when they think about a new career. It is also less of a barrier than it looks. Many careers do not expect experience in the field at all, and almost every job values skills you already use in a different setting.

This guide shows which careers hire people with no related experience, how to prove you can do the work anyway, and the seven steps that take you from first idea to first offer.

## How many careers need no experience?

O\*NET, the U.S. Department of Labor's career database, asks workers in each job how much related work experience a new hire needs. For **257 of the 875 careers** with full data on this site, the most common answer is **none**. That is about 29% of all careers.

These are not all entry-level jobs. Of the 234 no-experience careers with their own BLS pay figure, 102 pay more than the median for all U.S. workers ($50,980, BLS May 2025). Some examples with large workforces:

| Career | Median pay | Usual preparation |
|---|---|---|
| [Police and sheriff's patrol officers](/careers/police-and-sheriff-s-patrol-officers) | $76,210 | Academy training (Job Zone 3) |
| [Postal Service mail carriers](/careers/postal-service-mail-carriers) | $60,550 | On-the-job training (Job Zone 2) |
| [Firefighters](/careers/firefighters) | $59,280 | Fire academy (Job Zone 2) |

"No experience" does not mean "no training". Several of these jobs start with an academy, a license course or a short certificate. But they do not expect you to have done the job before, and the employer or program usually teaches you.

## Experience in the field vs. transferable skills

When a job ad says "experience required", it often means "proof you can do the core tasks". You can offer that proof without having held the title. O\*NET rates 25 skills, such as judgment, coordination and time management, in every career. Many of these show up across very different jobs:

- **Judgment and decision making** is rated 3 or higher (out of 5) in 672 of the 875 careers.
- **Time management** is rated 3 or higher in 648 careers.
- **Coordination** is rated 3 or higher in 628 careers.

If you have used these skills for years, you already have part of the experience the new job wants. Our [transferable skills list](/guides/transferable-skills-list) explains each one, and the [transferable skills finder](/transferable-skills) shows which careers rely on the ones you are strongest in.

## 7 steps to change careers with no experience

### 1. Start with careers close to what you do now

The shortest path into a new field starts from your current skills. Enter your job in the [career change tool](/career-change/tool) to see the 25 careers with the most similar skills, knowledge and daily work. Across all careers on this site, 88.2% of these close matches usually need no more preparation than the job the person already has.

### 2. Check what the new career really asks for

Open the career profile for each option and look at three things: the education most workers report, the training time, and whether a license or certificate is required. A job that "needs experience" in ads may only need a short certificate in practice.

### 3. Close the one gap that matters most

Most career changers have one main gap, not ten. It might be a software tool, a license, or a specific type of task. Pick the single gap that blocks you from applying and close it first: a short course, a certificate, or a small practice project.

### 4. Build proof with small projects

Proof beats promises. Ways to create it without a job title:

- volunteer for a nonprofit that needs the skill;
- take a freelance or part-time project;
- ask your current employer for a task that overlaps with the new field;
- build a small portfolio, such as sample reports, designs or lesson plans.

Two or three concrete examples are enough to move you from "no experience" to "some relevant experience".

### 5. Rewrite your resume around results

Lead with results that matter in the new job, not with your old title. "Cut processing time 20% by redesigning the intake form" shows problem solving to any employer. Our guide to the [career change resume](/guides/career-change-resume) has examples and a template.

### 6. Talk to people in the job

Short conversations with people already doing the work tell you what the job is really like and often lead to openings that are never posted. Ask for 20 minutes, ask specific questions, and follow up with thanks. Our guide to [informational interviews](/guides/informational-interviews) has a script.

### 7. Apply where new people are expected

Look for entry points that are built for newcomers: trainee programs, apprenticeships, academy classes, associate roles and "will train" listings. Large employers and public agencies often hire in groups and train new staff together.

## Mistakes that keep people stuck

- **Waiting until you feel fully qualified.** Many job ads list wishes, not requirements. If you meet most of the core tasks, apply.
- **Starting with the most distant career.** A move from teaching to software engineering is possible, but a move from teaching to corporate training is faster and teaches you how to switch.
- **Paying for long programs first.** Test the work with a short course or project before you commit to a degree.
- **Hiding your past career.** Your old job is where your transferable skills came from. Explain the connection.

## Common questions

### Can you really get a new job with no experience?

Yes. For 257 of 875 careers in O\*NET, the most common amount of related experience new workers need is none. Many of these jobs train you through an academy, apprenticeship or short program after you are hired.

### What jobs pay well with no experience?

Of the careers that usually need no related experience and have their own BLS pay figure, 102 pay above the U.S. median of $50,980. Examples include police officers ($76,210), mail carriers ($60,550) and firefighters ($59,280).

### How do I explain a career change with no experience in an interview?

Connect your past work to the new job's core tasks, give one or two concrete examples, and explain why you chose this field. See [how to answer "Why are you changing careers?"](/guides/why-are-you-changing-careers).

### Should I take an entry-level job to change careers?

Sometimes. If the new career pays well later and the entry job teaches the core skills, a short step back can pay off. First check whether a close match lets you switch without starting over.
