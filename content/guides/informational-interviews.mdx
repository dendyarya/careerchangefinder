An informational interview is a short conversation with someone who works in a career you are considering. You are not asking for a job. You are asking for information: what the work is really like, how they got in, and what they would do in your place.

For career changers, these conversations are the single most useful research tool. They tell you things no job ad or data table can, and they often lead to referrals and openings that are never posted.

## Why informational interviews work

- **You learn the real job.** Data shows tasks and pay; people tell you what a normal Tuesday feels like.
- **You test your fit cheaply.** Twenty minutes of conversation can save a year in the wrong program.
- **You get noticed by people, not filters.** Career changers often get screened out by software because their past titles do not match. A person who has met you reads your story differently.
- **You build a network in the new field.** The people you talk to know who is hiring.

## Who to ask

Aim for people one or two steps ahead of where you want to be, not the most senior person in the field. They remember what getting in was like.

Good places to find them:

1. **Your existing network.** Former colleagues, classmates, friends of friends. Ask: "Do you know anyone who works as a ___?"
2. **LinkedIn.** Search the job title plus your city. People who also changed careers are often the most willing to help.
3. **Alumni networks** from your school or college.
4. **Professional associations** and local meetups for the field.
5. **Instructors** in courses you are taking.

Start with warm contacts. A message that says "Maria suggested I reach out" is answered far more often than a cold one.

## How to ask: a message template

Keep it short, specific and easy to say yes to.

> Hi [Name],
>
> I'm a [current job] exploring a move into [new field], and I saw that you [specific detail: moved from teaching into instructional design / work as an HVAC technician in Ohio].
>
> Would you be open to a 20-minute call in the next few weeks? I'd love to hear how you got started and what you wish you had known. I'm not asking about job openings, just trying to understand the work.
>
> Thanks for considering it,
> [Your name]

Tips:

- Ask for 15 to 20 minutes, and stick to it.
- Offer to work around their schedule, by phone or video.
- If they do not reply, send one short follow-up after a week, then let it go.

## Prepare before the call

- Read about the career first. Know the basics: main tasks, typical pay, usual training. A career profile, such as the one for [dental hygienists](/careers/dental-hygienists), covers this in minutes.
- Look at the person's background so you can ask about their path.
- Write down five to eight questions and put them in order of importance.
- Have a 30-second summary of who you are and why you are interested.

## 20 good questions to ask

### About the work

1. What does a typical day or week look like?
2. Which tasks take most of your time?
3. What do you enjoy most about the work?
4. What is the hardest part of the job?
5. What surprised you when you started?

### About getting in

6. How did you get into this field?
7. What training or certificates mattered most?
8. What do employers look for in new hires?
9. Are there entry-level or trainee roles that career changers can apply for?
10. What would you do in my position?

### About skills

11. Which skills from my background would be most useful here?
12. What skills would I need to build first?
13. How do people usually learn the job: courses, on-the-job training, apprenticeships?

### About the field

14. How is the field changing?
15. Which employers in the area hire the most people for this role?
16. What do pay and schedules look like for new people versus experienced ones?
17. What kinds of people do well in this work, and who struggles?

### Closing

18. Is there anything I should read or follow to learn more?
19. Is there anyone else you think I should talk to?
20. May I stay in touch as I make progress?

Question 19 is the most valuable. One good conversation usually leads to two more.

## During the conversation

- Let them talk. Aim for them speaking 80% of the time.
- Take brief notes.
- Watch the time and offer to end when the 20 minutes are up.
- Do not ask for a job. If they offer to pass on your resume, accept with thanks.

## After the call

1. **Send a thank-you message the same day.** Mention one specific thing you learned.
2. **Act on their advice** and tell them later: "I took the course you suggested."
3. **Follow up on introductions** they offered, and thank them again when you do.
4. **Keep a simple log** of who you talked to, what you learned and when to check in next.

## Sample thank-you note

> Hi [Name],
>
> Thank you for taking the time to talk with me today. Your advice to [specific tip, e.g. start with a medical billing certificate before applying to hospital roles] was exactly what I needed, and I've already signed up for the course.
>
> I'll let you know how it goes. Thanks again for your help.
>
> [Your name]

Keep it short, specific and sent the same day. If they offered an introduction, add one line: "I'd be grateful for the introduction to [Name] whenever it's convenient."

## Turning conversations into a decision

After three to five conversations about the same career, review your notes:

- What did everyone mention as the hardest part, and can you live with it?
- What did they say new people need, and how far are you from it?
- Did anyone say they would not choose the career again, and why?

If the answers line up with what you want, move on to applications. If not, you have saved yourself a costly mistake.

## Common questions

### Is it rude to ask for an informational interview?

No. Many people enjoy talking about their work and remember being helped themselves. Keep the request short, respect their time, and do not ask for a job.

### How many informational interviews should I do?

Three to five per career you are seriously considering is a good target. After that, the answers start to repeat, and you will know whether the career fits.

### Should I bring my resume?

Have it ready, but do not send it unless they ask. The purpose is learning, not applying.

### What if no one responds?

Try warmer contacts first, through friends, alumni or former colleagues, and make your message more specific. A message that mentions a shared connection or a detail of their work is answered far more often.
