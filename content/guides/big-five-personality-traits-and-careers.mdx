The Big Five is the most widely used model of personality in psychology research. It describes personality with five broad traits, each measured on a scale from low to high. Unlike personality "types", the Big Five does not sort people into boxes: everyone has some of each trait, in different amounts.

Your personality does not decide which careers you can do, but it affects which work settings feel natural and which feel draining. This guide explains each trait and the kinds of careers that tend to suit high and low levels of it.

## The five traits

The model is often remembered with the letters OCEAN. The names below are the ones used by the International Personality Item Pool (IPIP), which our free test is built on.

| Trait | What it describes |
|---|---|
| Extraversion | How much you seek out people, activity and attention |
| Agreeableness | How much you focus on cooperation, trust and others' needs |
| Conscientiousness | How organized, careful and dependable you tend to be |
| Emotional stability | How calm you stay under stress (the opposite end is often called neuroticism) |
| Openness (intellect or imagination) | How much you enjoy new ideas, experiences and abstract thinking |

Each trait is a spectrum. Scores in the middle are common and mean you can adapt in both directions.

## What each trait means at work

### Extraversion

- **Higher:** energized by meetings, customers and teamwork. Often comfortable leading groups and speaking up.
- **Lower:** prefers focused, independent work and fewer interruptions. Often thinks before speaking and works well alone.
- **Careers to consider:** higher scores often suit sales, management, teaching and public relations; lower scores often suit research, technical, analytical and many trade roles. See [jobs for introverts](/careers/personality/introverts) and [jobs for extroverts](/careers/personality/extroverts).

### Agreeableness

- **Higher:** cooperative, patient, focused on helping and keeping harmony.
- **Lower:** direct, competitive, comfortable with conflict and tough decisions.
- **Careers to consider:** higher scores often suit healthcare, counseling, social services and education; lower scores often suit negotiation-heavy, competitive or critical roles, such as some sales, legal or auditing work. See [careers for people who like helping](/careers/personality/helping-people) and [competitive careers](/careers/personality/competitive).

### Conscientiousness

- **Higher:** organized, reliable, careful with details and deadlines.
- **Lower:** flexible, spontaneous, comfortable with changing priorities.
- **Careers to consider:** higher scores often suit accounting, compliance, healthcare, engineering and project management; lower scores often suit creative, fast-changing or varied work. See [detail-oriented careers](/careers/personality/detail-oriented) and [careers for people who hate routine](/careers/personality/hate-routine).

### Emotional stability

- **Higher:** calm under pressure, recovers quickly from setbacks.
- **Lower:** feels stress more strongly; often more sensitive to risks and problems.
- **Careers to consider:** higher scores often suit emergency services, critical care and high-stakes roles; people with lower scores may prefer steadier, predictable environments. See [high-pressure careers](/careers/personality/high-pressure) and [low-stress careers](/careers/personality/low-stress).

### Openness

- **Higher:** curious, imaginative, enjoys new ideas and learning.
- **Lower:** practical, prefers proven methods and concrete tasks.
- **Careers to consider:** higher scores often suit research, design, writing and strategy; lower scores often suit practical, hands-on or procedural work. See [creative careers](/careers/personality/creative) and [hands-on careers](/careers/personality/hands-on).

## How the career lists are built

O\*NET, the U.S. Department of Labor's career database, does not rate careers on the Big Five directly. It does rate each career on related qualities, such as how much contact with others the work involves, how much attention to detail it needs, how stressful it is, and how much it calls for cooperation or innovation. The career lists linked on this page use those ratings, so they point to careers whose daily demands fit each trait level.

## Personality is one input, not a verdict

Research links personality traits to job satisfaction and performance, but the links are moderate. Many introverts are excellent salespeople, and many highly agreeable people are tough negotiators when they need to be. Use your results to understand which environments will feel easier, and combine them with:

- **your interests,** measured by the [career test](/career-test), which uses the O\*NET Interest Profiler;
- **your skills,** which the [career change tool](/career-change/tool) compares with every other career;
- **your practical needs,** such as pay, schedule and location.

## Using your results in a career change

1. **Compare your traits with your current job's demands.** If you score low on extraversion and your job is constant customer contact, that mismatch may explain why the work drains you.
2. **Use traits to choose between similar options.** If two careers match your skills equally, the one that fits your personality is likely to feel easier day to day.
3. **Look at the work setting, not just the title.** The same career can be very different by employer: a quiet hospital lab and a busy emergency department both employ lab and medical staff.
4. **Plan around the hard parts.** If you score lower on emotional stability but want a demanding career, look for employers with strong support, predictable schedules or team-based work.
5. **Recheck after a few years.** Traits shift a little with age and experience, and your preferences may change with them.

Personality is most useful as a filter after you have a shortlist from your interests and skills, not as the first step.

## Common questions

### Which Big Five trait matters most for career success?

Across many studies, conscientiousness is the trait most consistently linked to job performance in a wide range of jobs. But the best fit depends on the career: extraversion matters more in sales and management, openness in creative and research roles.

### Is the Big Five better than Myers-Briggs?

They measure personality differently. The Big Five uses continuous scales and is the standard model in personality research. Myers-Briggs sorts people into 16 types. If you want a research-based measure, the Big Five is the usual choice.

### Can personality change over time?

Somewhat. Research shows that traits are fairly stable in adults but tend to shift gradually with age, for example becoming more conscientious and emotionally stable over time.

### Should I choose a career based on personality?

Use it as one factor. Personality helps explain which work settings suit you, but interests, skills and practical needs matter at least as much.
